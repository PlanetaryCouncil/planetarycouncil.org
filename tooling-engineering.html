A note on authorship

The artist has not read this book. He was busy creating. He asked that the original spirit be preserved, said to trust Opus 5 with it, and left.

So the deal is this: every word in the gold monospace is his, exactly as he typed or dictated it — slips, typos, arrows, all of it. Everything in this serif is mine: the chapters, the lessons, the claims about what he was doing and why. That reading is unreviewed by him. To check it against the source, the complete unedited record of all 98 exchanges is at the end of the book.

The genre has a name now: promptbook. You read one for the same reason you read a chess annotation — not for the moves, but for the mind choosing them. What follows is a real transcript from a real project, a satirical music video called Vote for the Bin, which ended up as a deterministic audiovisual engine that listens to its own pixels. The artefact is on the internet. The interesting thing is how it got there — and the whole tape is printed at the back.

The prevailing story about working with AI is prompt engineering: find the incantation, get the output. This transcript shows something else, and the artist named it himself when I asked what to call the book. He didn't say prompt log. He said tooling engineering.

The difference is everything. He almost never asked me for an output. He asked me for instruments — a lyric aligner with two keyboard layouts for different hands, a strategy file to hold ideas before any of them were chosen, a reframe studio that exports MP4 without a screen recorder. Then he used the instruments himself, at speed, with taste I could not supply. The model was never the craftsman. It was the machine shop.

He is not a programmer. He is a tool user — which turns out to be the scarcer skill.

Chapter one · The correction

Say it wrong, then say it exactly

His single most-used move: let me be wrong, quickly, then delete the wrongness with one sentence.

Most people either over-specify a first request until it is a legal document, or accept a bad first answer to avoid confrontation. He does neither. First drafts arrive loose — often literally dictated while walking. Then, when I misread, the correction is surgical, and it is never a repeat of the original request. It names the exact misconception.

Bro... The folder with lyrics. { "t": 39.32, "w": "A" }, { "t": 39.621, "w": "[I]" } ----> simply... Word AI / A becomes together with I / So special cases, dash was to indicate prolongated words, not special magic
ClaudeGot it — I over-read the syntax. Correcting my model: [...] = join, the word continues, no space. - = prolongation — you typing a held sound. Plain text. No mechanism, no magic. I had described the dash as if it were a token. It isn't — it's typography.

LessonModels invent grammar where humans use punctuation. He didn't re-explain the format; he named the false belief — "not special magic" — and the whole wrong branch died in one line.

did you remember such a fun mistake because I thought that it is obvious when we talk about the width. And it seems like this frame size is horizontal, which is which is weird. I can't believe that the Frontier AI model could misinterpret the intention. Like, how how is that even possible? I just don't get it.
ClaudeFair hit — that dark horizontal letterbox is the whisper zone, and it's horizontal because I normalised both axes by the width. On a 16:9 stage, 22% of 1920 is 422px — which eats 78% of the vertical span but only 44% of the horizontal. "10% of the frame" obviously means 10% of each edge's own dimension. I hard-coded a landscape assumption instead.

LessonHe describes the symptom he can see, not the fix he imagines. A stray horizontal band is a fact; "use per-axis normalisation" would have been a guess. Symptoms are cheap for him and diagnostic for me.

LessonHe audits the reasoning, not just the result — and he does it laughing. The "LOL" matters: he keeps the pressure on the work and off the collaboration, which is why sixty-three days of blunt correction never curdled.

Chapter two · The archive

Never overwrite the past

The rule that made ~60 versions of one file a feature instead of a mess.

Early on I improved a file in place, the way software engineers are trained to. He stopped it immediately, and it became the single most consequential process rule of the project.

you should make it ameba_01 _02 etc...
I prefer when the file names increase, append only, we continously expand... And then when too many options ----> just new naming pattern, new branch. My favourite so far: MINDFUCK_01. But too much mindfuck over the central part of the image. … The first version was the best, actually. Maybe we should just call it ___visualizer_wrapping_up.
ClaudeNew branch, new name, append-only within it: ___visualizer_wrapping_up_01.html — built on the MINDFUCK line's punch, but with the energy wrapped around the frame.

LessonGit branches are for code. This is for taste. Because nothing was ever overwritten, he could say "the first version was the best" on day 19 and it was still there, byte-identical, to be forked from. Version control preserves history; this preserves options.

The naming carries information too — MINDFUCK_05 versus GRADIENT_WINGS_02 versus wrapping_up_02. Numbers alone lose meaning across a week; the name says which idea the branch is about. When a lineage accumulated too many toggles, he started a fresh name at _01, copied from the current best. Sedimentary, not destructive.

Chapter three · The interrogation

Ask what the thing is actually for

Two questions, thirty seconds each, that deleted more code than any refactor.

He asks about his own project as if he were auditing someone else's. These are not gotchas — they are genuine curiosity, and they repeatedly found dead weight I had been maintaining without noticing.

why do we keep scenes count in the first place? does it change anything? curious...
ClaudeGood question — and the honest answer for the version you're recording is almost nothing, which surprised me too until I traced it. Cuts pick the field language and set per-scene intensity — but I had deliberately disabled the biggest thing they used to do, so most of that pipeline was now inert.

LessonAn AI will happily maintain a system nobody uses, forever, because it was asked to build it once. "Does it change anything?" is the cheapest audit in existence, and only the human ever thinks to run it.

How do you measure intensity now? Through sounds or visual?
ClaudeLet me trace the exact source rather than answer from memory. 100% sound. Zero pixels. Intensity never looks at the video image at all — it reads the stem envelope from the baked feature rows. The video is never sampled for this.

LessonThis question is the hinge of the entire artwork. The answer — that the engine was deaf to its own picture — is what produced the next chapter, and eventually the one claim the finished piece can make that no other music video can.

Chapter four · The registry

Keep the ideas, not the answer

The most sophisticated instruction in the transcript, dictated out loud, mid-thought.

Having learned the engine only listened to audio, he wanted it to listen to the film as well. A normal request would be "make it react to the video." What he actually asked for was a place to put ideas that aren't chosen yet — and explicitly forbade wiring any of it into the artwork.

So energy split should be also based on the... not just audio, but also on the video … maybe you can judge it by the amount of contrast, by the amount of pixels. Let's establish a new metric for how dynamic the video is. And this is my idea, and we'll have a thousands of ideas. So just keep the ideas in the... like, a, you know, JSON strategy file with different ideas, with different metrics before we nail something that works very, very reliably. And the first idea is to split the screen in multiple squares and just calculate the different palettes on different squares … how many mind fuck items are in the single frame
ClaudeThe right move is exactly what you said: don't wire it into the artwork yet. A strategy registry to accumulate metric ideas, plus a reusable frame processor that computes the first one — and works on a single still, too, for later. Nine ideas registered, six implemented as channels on a 10×10 grid.

LessonHe separated having the idea from committing to it, and made that separation a file. Nothing shipped that day; the registry outlived a dozen versions and the metric that eventually shipped was chosen from it later, on evidence.

"How many mind fuck items are in the single frame" is, unimprovably, a specification for per-cell visual entropy. It became grid_busyness. It turned out the picture and the audio agreed only weakly — a correlation of about 0.11 — which is precisely why fusing them was worth doing: two nearly independent senses, not one signal counted twice.

The sound decides when.
The picture decides how much.

Chapter five · The override

Your hands beat my inference

The moment he was right and I was confidently, expensively wrong.

A video file refused to import into his editor. I had a clean theory and evidence for it. He had already tried the thing my theory depended on, and said so plainly.

HAndbrake file? I tried encode with handbrake twice before asking you. When I tried with new project at 30 fps same issue persists... Maybe I should work with a different software... Da Vinci resolve? I'm just so tired to learn a new software right now.
ClaudeThat changes everything — if HandBrake failed too, then it's not the files. Every encoder failing, including one whose output is already sitting in your timeline, points at the application's state rather than the media. [New empty project, exact matching format — same failure. It parsed the container fine and failed at decode.] That's a decoder problem in the app.

LessonI had weighted my own inference above his lived experience. He didn't argue the theory — he stated what he had already done, which is unfalsifiable and immediately reorganised the whole diagnosis. Say what you tried. It is the highest-value sentence a human can contribute.

Chapter six · The verdict

Name the masterpiece, kill the furniture

He spent a week building features, then deleted most of them in one paragraph.

The visualiser had waveforms, histograms, particle fields, an amoeba mode. All of it was working. Then he watched it properly and delivered the judgement that reorganised the project permanently.

I actually think that this RGB split … the waveforms take a lot of space. Histograms takes lots of space. But the RGB split that is connected to stems and changes shapes and the depth and different perspectives and mirrors and morphing. Like, this is a proper mind … such an anesthesia experience with sounds and the text and this is a mind blowing masterpiece.
ClaudeNoted for good: the field language is the artwork; the anatomy layers are furniture. The waveforms and histograms were always diagnostics wearing costumes. It also explains why PURE lands so hard — it's the first version that gives the field nothing to compete with.

LessonOnly the human can say which part is the art. I can tell you the histogram is correct; I cannot tell you it is furniture. He wrote that verdict into my long-term memory, and every version after it obeyed a rule I would never have derived on my own.

Note the shape of the instruction: he did not say "remove the waveforms." He said which thing is the point. That is a durable rule; a deletion is a one-time task. Everything downstream — including three whole branches built weeks later — followed from it automatically.

Chapter seven · The instruments

Ask for the tool, not the result

Why this book is called tooling engineering.

Here is the pattern in its purest form. Each of these requests could have been "please do X for me." Each was instead "build me the thing that lets me do X, forever."

I have a horizontal video … I want to have it repurposed for the social media, so I have to have vertical. Please make me, like, an intro that I can just upload the video, and then I can export it. And, ideally, so I can export it directly from a browser. I don't want to record the screen and then re encode it.
ClaudeA different tool entirely — not a visualiser, a reframe studio. Let me check what export codecs actually exist before I promise MP4. [MediaRecorder supports MP4/H.264 here.] A real .mp4 download, no screen recording, no re-encode. First export came out suspiciously small — the background tab had suspended the animation frame loop, so one frame got captured. Rebuilt on a timer, and added an honest warning when frames are being dropped rather than silently shipping a broken file.

LessonHe specified the constraint that mattered to his life — no screen recording, no second encode — rather than the implementation. Constraints like that are impossible to guess and trivial to state.

I want to have a tool that will try to align lyrics.... I'll load text from suno and stems. Timeline with song and below timeline with vocals. Should be so much easier. I remmber some keyboard shortcuts were inconvenient for my hand, so please have 2 presets of shorcuts for different hands. Finally, when I pres "E" while hovering on a word, it splits the word into half and split the letters in half, I'll finetune manually later if needed. Thanks bro, easy job
ClaudeYour own tool, your own rules — and E produces the project's own append grammar, so a split word still renders as one: TECHNOLOGY → TECHN + [OLOGY]. Both stems decode in 0.7s, and the number that matters: the mix is 99.7% active, the vocals only 21.8% — sparse, with clear silence between phrases. You'll see every onset.

LessonLook at what he specified: a second waveform so the eye can find the vocal onsets, and two keyboard layouts because his hand got tired. That is ergonomics, from someone who knows he will press these keys two hundred times. No model volunteers that. Only the person doing the work knows where the work hurts.

The same instinct produced a burpee-sync tool for a video he shot in his garden, a crop-and-orient studio, a countdown recorder, and an achievements format that lives inside the lyrics file so it can be hand-edited in a text editor. When a tool came back over-built, he trimmed it in one line — "There's no square here. Just two options" — and never once apologised for changing his mind.

Chapter eight · The release

Let it be alive, then stop

Determinism was a choice. He un-chose it, and the artwork improved.

For weeks the engine was rigorously deterministic — seek anywhere, get an identical frame. I had treated an unseeded random field as a bug and logged it as debt. He read the debt note and overruled it.

might have some randomness. Maybe some randomness will be better. The fact that it is deterministic is actually a random choice, honestly. I do not mind some unexpected renders … I'm an easy person. First render is always the best. … They do not know which buttons to press. So by default they have a loader, and after every load there is a countdown three to one, and there is a menu open by default … in the bottom right corner is, like, a big letter M pulsing that says press M to hide the menu.
Claude"First render is always the best" is now official policy — and it fits: every visitor gets their own roll of the field. Debt closed, no seeding. Shipped the visitor flow to the live page: Play → 3·2·1 → playing, menu open, pulsing M teaching the one key that matters.

LessonTwo decisions in one dictation. He converted a defect into the product's best property — nobody can rewatch this piece, only revisit it — and in the same breath solved the stranger's first ten seconds, which is the only part of a release most builders never see with fresh eyes.

And then, on 22 July, mid-flight, with a dozen open ideas: "I've got something majestic — no more changes." He moved to the cover photo, the description, the SRT subtitles, the one-page promo. Knowing the exact hour to stop building is not a technical skill and cannot be delegated.

Ten rules from the tape

Extracted from 98 exchanges. None of them are about wording.

  1. 01
    Ask for the instrument"Build me the tool that does X" outlives "do X" by a hundred uses.
  2. 02
    Append, never overwriteNumbered files, descriptive branch names. Yesterday's version is the one you'll want on Friday.
  3. 03
    Correct the belief, not the output"Not special magic" kills an entire wrong branch. Re-explaining the spec does not.
  4. 04
    Report symptoms, not fixes"There's a weird horizontal band" is data. Your guessed cause is noise.
  5. 05
    Say what you already triedIt is unfalsifiable and it instantly reorganises the diagnosis.
  6. 06
    Ask what it's for"Does it change anything?" finds the dead systems the model will maintain forever.
  7. 07
    Park ideas in a fileSeparate having an idea from committing to it. Choose later, on evidence.
  8. 08
    Name which part is the artA durable verdict beats a hundred deletions, and only you can issue it.
  9. 09
    State the constraint from your life"No screen recording." "My hand gets tired." Unguessable, trivial to say.
  10. 10
    Know the hour to stop"No more changes" is a decision, not a failure of ambition.

Notice what is missing. No role-play preambles, no "you are an expert," no temperature tuning, no chain-of-thought incantations. Nothing in this list would have read as advanced advice in 1975 to a machinist with a good apprentice. That is rather the point.

The frontier moves on its own; the models get better without anyone's help. What does not arrive automatically is a person who knows which of the sixty versions was the masterpiece, that the histograms were furniture, that the shortcut should sit under a tired left hand, and that the correct number of further changes is zero. Those judgements are the scarce input — and they compound, because every one of them made the next hour of machine work land somewhere true.

That is why the human stays ahead:
not by out-typing the model, but by owning the taste, the archive, and the stop.
