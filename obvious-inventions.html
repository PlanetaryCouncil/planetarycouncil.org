A short note on technology & fear

Obvious inventions don't build fear.

Almost every new technology arrives with a panic attached. Writing, the telegraph, television, AI — all of them scared somebody. But a small set of inventions slipped into the world to nothing but a shrug and a thank you. What do they have in common?

The ones nobody feared

Anesthesia1846
Surgeons adopted ether almost overnight. When the alternative is screaming through an amputation, the invention explains itself.
LED lighting2000s
Replaced a 130-year-old technology with essentially zero resistance. The only complaint was color temperature.
The zipper1913
Buttons, but faster. No sermons were preached against it.
The safety bicycle1880s
Triggered a craze, not a panic. People could immediately see what it was for.
The shipping container1956
Quietly rewired the entire global economy. It's just a box — and everyone could see it was just a box.
GPS for everyone1990s
Never getting lost again needed no explanation and raised no alarm — until it was welded to phones, decades later.

The common pattern

These inventions share one quality: they are obvious. Not obvious to invent — obvious to understand. The moment you see one, you know exactly what it does, who it helps, and what it replaces. Your first thought isn't "what will this do to us?" but "why on earth didn't this exist before?"

Fear needs a gap. A gap between what a technology does and what we can imagine it doing. Writing, television, the internet, AI — these mediate between people, and their consequences are open-ended, so imagination rushes in and fills the gap with dread.

A zipper mediates between you and your jacket. There is no gap. The whole invention fits in a single glance — its purpose, its limits, its worst-case scenario. When a thing is that self-evident, there's nothing left over for fear to grow in.

So the rule of thumb: the more obvious an invention feels, the less fear it creates. Obvious inventions solve a pain everyone already knew they had. The frightening ones create possibilities nobody asked for — and nobody can yet see the edges of.
